One example — receivables

Accuracy needs granularity. Granularity needs an engine.

Without granularity — what a budget-P&L-to-cash does

One balance, one DSO, one curve.

$ 198.7M receivables× 73 days group DSO= one collection curve
  • 73 is an average of 69, 80, 77 and 84 — the four entities in the same ledger. And of 40 to 120 by client. Nobody actually pays at 73.
  • Averaging destroys the one thing a cash forecast is for: timing. Fast payers are pushed later, slow ones pulled earlier. The total is right; every month is wrong.
  • A client that changes its terms is invisible — it moves the group average by a day and the forecast by nothing.
Quick to build. Not much sense to it.
With granularity — what makes sense

Same balance, 360 curves, added up.

4 ent × 6 BU × 15 clients× 360 measured DSOs= 360 curves, added
  • Each cell's DSO is measured, not typed — per entity, BU and counterparty, from settlement history. K-Φ computes and segregates them; they sit there as rule inputs.
  • One rule, written once, applied at the grain you choose — "collect AR at measured DSO, scope entity × counterparty". The engine runs the 360; nobody keys them.
  • A change shows up where it happens — one client slipping to 120 days moves one curve, in the right month.
This is where the forecast is right — and it costs the analyst nothing.

The objection — “I already forecast from AR and AP”

Three things an AR/AP forecast cannot see.

1

Half the cash flow.

AR and AP are two subledgers. Payroll, tax, debt service, capex, intercompany — the flows that decide the quarter — are in the ledger and nowhere else.

2

Past the invoices it holds.

Both methods hold the same invoices. AR can only run them off: once the last one is collected, around sixty days, the curve is at zero — it has no way to generate next week's invoice. The ledger's revenue run-rate projects the next ones through the measured DSO, and the budget takes over when the run-rate fades.

3

When the cash lands.

A due date is what was agreed. A 3-month DSO on a segmented perimeter — entity, BU, counterparty — is what actually happens. Measured, not declared.

And the parts that come after a forecast — reconciling to the statements, hedging the exposures, replacing budget with actuals close by close — need a coherent accounting base underneath. A list of due dates has none. The ledger is one.
